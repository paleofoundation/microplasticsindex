# Food storage primary-source review

Review date: October 22, 2026. Scope: the five assigned MP3 PDFs plus one contextual methods article. Companion extraction: `food-storage-reviewed.json`.

All six SHA-256 hashes match `MP3/papers.csv`. PDF title and DOI were checked against the manifest. Relevant complete methods and results pages were read. Eleven rendered primary pages were inspected to check table columns, graph denominators, captions, and text extraction. Supporting information was not supplied or retrieved. This is a bounded evidence review, not a systematic review or publication approval.

## Proposed acceptance

Accept the listed original food-wrap and PS-container findings only with the exact conditions, denominators, size windows, and limitations. Keep PP-box nanoplastic findings qualitative. Exclude the laboratory reference-material generation paper from consumer release counts. The missing LDPE quality-control supplement, consumer zip-bag coverage, generic reusable tub coverage, and regulatory synthesis remain HOLD.

The public opening can say: Laboratory studies have detected plastic particles released from some food wraps and containers. Reported amounts depend on the material, liquid, temperature, contact time, and detection method. These studies do not establish a common release rate for all food storage products or a measured human health outcome.

## Strongest usable storage findings

**LDPE food wrap, Wang et al., 2024.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.4c05504. One commercial film was cut to 6 × 6 cm and immersed in 40 mL ultrapure water. Counts were normalized to film area. Nile Red staining, laser confocal scanning microscopy, and ImageJ counted particles in the 10-5000 µm window. Bulk film FTIR and Raman characterization should not be described as individual-particle confirmation.

| Condition | Paper-reported count |
| --- | --- |
| Microwave, 119 W, 2 min; final water temperature 48 °C | 147 MPs/cm² |
| Microwave, 119 W, 10 min; final water temperature 96 °C | 313 MPs/cm² |
| Refrigerator, 4 °C, 7 days | 103 MPs/cm² |
| Refrigerator, 4 °C, 30 days | 72 MPs/cm² |
| Freezer, -20 °C, 30 days | 66 MPs/cm² |
| Freezer, -20 °C, 60 days | 112 MPs/cm² |
| Two freeze-thaw cycles | 132 MPs/cm² |
| Four freeze-thaw cycles | 183 MPs/cm² |

Each cycle was 24 h at -20 °C followed by 24 h at 25 °C. These are paper-reported point estimates; the supplied main text does not give numeric SD or SE for these particular counts. Do not infer a monotonic time trend from the refrigerator results. Do not convert these into particles per meal or bag.

Locators: PDF pp. 4-5, printed C-D, §§2.1-2.3; PDF p. 7, printed F, §3.1. The supplied repository file has an added cover page. Its untreated-film control was immersed for 1 h at room temperature. Full quality control, procedural blanks, and statistical details are deferred to supporting Text S7 in §2.7, PDF p. 7. That supplement was not inspected. The source is accessible through a Dutch article 25fa repository deposit with rights retained, so link the DOI and paraphrase rather than republishing figures or the PDF.

**PS instant-noodle container, Wang et al., 2023.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e15787. The pH experiment reported 36 PS particles/container with 200 mL solution at pH 9, maintained at 100 °C for 6 h. That is an extreme test condition, not normal room-temperature food storage. The paper reports mean values and corresponding SD from at least three repeats; an exact SD was not transcribed. µ-FTIR mapping covered the full filter. Comparison bins began at 10-100 µm, and particles were predominantly in that bin. The 0.2 µm filtration pore is not a validated µ-FTIR detection floor. The paper reports no MPs in procedure blanks and PS/PE validation recoveries of 91.4%/81.9%.

Locators: PDF p. 3 §2.2, p. 4 §2.3, p. 6 Figure 3, p. 7 §3.1. Critical correction: the abstract and pH discussion call 36 the highest count, but Figure 3(c) shows a higher result at 8 h. Describe 36 only as the result at the stated condition or the highest in the pH series. Do not call it the maximum across all experiments. Do not estimate a new exact 8 h count from the plot. PS, PE, and PP were detected, but the 36 figure is PS only. CC BY 4.0.

## Related evidence requiring narrower treatment

**Nanoplastic scenarios, Wang et al., Toxics, 2023.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/toxics11070550. A PP takeaway box was exposed to 500 mL water at 100 °C for 20 min. GCPP and MCPP bags were heat-sealing materials for composite packaging tested at 121 °C for 20 min. These are not consumer zip-top storage bags. After intensive pretreatment, sequential filtration selected nominal 50-100, 100-200, and 200-400 nm fractions. SEM, SERS, and a laser particle size analyzer were used; subsequent ultrasound can affect size and dispersibility. Ultrapure-water controls showed no NPs by SEM. No numeric release concentration or count with a suitable denominator was identified for the article table. Aggregation means filtration fractions do not describe all dispersed sizes. Figure 5b's GCPP label is 167 nm while the prose says 168 nm, so omit that figure. Non-detection in the MCPP scenario cannot establish safety. Locators: PDF pp. 3-5 §§2.2-3.1, p. 4 Table 1 and Figure 2, pp. 6-9 Figures 3-6. CC BY 4.0.

**Experimental NP standard, Wang et al., Molecules, 2023.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28217254. Exclude from exposure-rate comparisons. The laboratory method deliberately makes irregular reference particles using chemical/enzyme preparation, UV exposure, cutting, and mortar grinding. The 50-100, 100-200, and 200-400 nm fractions are generated standards, not ordinary storage release rates. A boiling-water comparison group supplies morphology context, not a household release dose. Locators: PDF pp. 7-8 §§3.4-3.5; p. 9 conclusions. CC BY 4.0.

## Gaps and editorial boundaries

- No primary consumer zip-top bag experiment was reviewed in this set. Do not generalize industrial heat-sealing film results to zip bags.
- No population of reusable household tubs or repeated-use tub experiment was reviewed. A PP takeaway box is related container evidence only.
- Do not pool these measurements. Their polymers, source materials, treatments, count denominators, and measurement windows differ.
- Keep styrene monomer, dissolved organic matter, and particle counts separate. A monomer concentration is not a particle concentration.
- Do not derive a human intake estimate, disease claim, or product ranking from these release experiments.
- Papers referencing EU food-contact migration regulations do not prove a current microplastic particle limit. Regulatory status requires separate verified primary instruments and remains outside this subtask.
- Primary identifiers and hashes, all useful listed values, and method-specific restrictions are preserved in the JSON. The parent scientific reviewer retains final acceptance authority.

## Added methods context: Cella et al., 2022

Cella, La Spina, Mehn, Fumagalli, Ceccone, Valsesia, and Gilliland. *Detecting Micro- and Nanoplastics Released from Food Packaging: Challenges and Analytical Strategies*. Polymers 14, 1238. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/polym14061238. File `MP3/729cb4e7c14706bd.pdf`; SHA-256 `729cb4e7c14706bd6d2e0e4ac52baa73d09e230b18bb1a36618489d9a03f77cb`, matching the manifest. CC BY 4.0. This is an original experimental Article, not a review, studying PE rice cooking bags, PE ice-cube bags, and nylon teabags. It combines Raman chemical identification, XPS, SEM, and calibrated FTIR using KBr pellets. It describes procedural blanks and unheated controls, cleaned glass/metal, and efforts to limit laboratory contamination. Relevant complete pages: PDF pp. 1-6 and 11. Page 11 was also visually inspected.

Use it to explain why method choice and sample preparation matter. The authors show that concentration, drying, heating, cooling, and aggregation can change what the analyst sees. A polymer mass result does not automatically establish how many particles were released, and simple spotting is insufficient to quantify particle numbers. This is useful support for keeping microscopy counts, polymer mass, and dissolved/oligomeric material separate, and for resisting comparisons across unlike size windows. No numerical release findings were transcribed or accepted in this contextual addition; ice-cube bags and cooking bags still do not fill the consumer zip-top storage-bag gap.

Additional original bag findings: With commercial PE ice-cube bags filled with 300 mL water, frozen at -20 °C for 24 h, manually emptied, and thawed in glass, Raman analysis identified PE particles mixed with ultramarine blue pigment and separate pigment particles. A room-temperature bag-contact control showed few visible particles, mainly PE. With commercial PE rice-cooking bags emptied of rice and boiled in 250 mL water at 100 °C for 10 min, Raman identified PE alongside residual rice material; the boiled-water blank showed neither PE nor rice particles. Locators: PDF p. 3 §2.2; pp. 6-7 Figure 1A-B and discussion. The rice-bag PE mass was below the KBr-pellet FTIR detection limit (p. 9). No numeric particle release rate is supplied here for either PE-bag example. The filtration pore is not a validated Raman analytical floor. These findings support a carefully labeled cooking/ice-bag subsection while leaving zip-top storage bags explicitly unreviewed.
